# Off-Market Sourcing Playbook

Nothing usable is listed. Fine — in rural Iowa most land never gets listed
anyway. Roughly half of Iowa farmland sales happen privately or at auction,
not through listings. The market exists; it's just invisible from the MLS.
This playbook is organized from highest-leverage to most creative.

---

## Tier 1 — Data-driven origination (do this first, it's free)

### 1. Build the parcel universe from county GIS
Almost every Iowa county publishes parcel ownership through Beacon
(Schneider Geospatial) or Vanguard Appraisals portals. Cross-reference with
transmission line maps (see [iowa-data-sources.md](iowa-data-sources.md)).
Output: every parcel within the transmission buffer, with owner name and tax
mailing address. This is the master list everything else works from.

### 2. Absentee-owner extraction
Filter the master list for tax mailing addresses outside the county (or out
of state). Absentee owners — heirs in Denver, retired farmers in Arizona —
have weaker emotional ties, are often already renting the ground out, and
respond well to letters. This is consistently the highest-yield cold
outreach segment.

### 3. Estate, trust, and life-event signals
- **Probate filings** at the county clerk of court — estates with farmland
  often need liquidity for taxes or to split among heirs.
- **Trust-owned parcels** ("XYZ Family Trust") — a trustee's job is fiduciary,
  not sentimental; a strong offer gets evaluated on its merits.
- **Recent farmland auctions in the area where a parcel got NO-SALED** —
  the owner already decided to sell and the market told them no. They're the
  warmest cold call in the county. Auction houses publish results.
- **CRP contract expirations** — ground coming out of the Conservation
  Reserve Program forces a decision (re-enroll, farm it, or sell). USDA
  publishes county-level CRP data; local FSA office chatter fills in the rest.

### 4. Tax-stress signals
County treasurer's delinquent tax lists and the annual tax sale. Not to buy
liens — to identify owners under financial pressure who might welcome a
clean, well-priced exit.

## Tier 2 — Relationship channels (the real Iowa land market)

### 5. Farm management companies
In Iowa a huge share of farmland — especially absentee- and trust-owned — is
run by professional farm managers: **Hertz Farm Management, Peoples Company,
Farmers National Company, Stalcup Ag Service**, plus regional bank trust
departments. One farm manager can represent dozens of owners in a county and
knows exactly which ones are open to selling. Pitch them as a channel, not a
one-off: "we're evaluating ground for an energy campus; if any of your
clients would entertain an offer or an option, we pay full commission and
close clean."

### 6. Local brokers — retained as buyer's scouts
Instead of asking brokers "what's listed" (nothing), retain one or two
county-level land brokers on a buyer-side fee to *originate*. They know who
just lost a tenant, who's fighting with siblings over the home place, and
who said at the co-op that they'd sell "for the right number." A modest
retainer plus success fee turns their gossip network into deal flow.

### 7. Auction houses
Sullivan Auctioneers, Steffes Group, and county-level auctioneers see
sellers before anyone. Ask them to bring pre-auction opportunities — sellers
often prefer a certain private sale over auction risk, and auctioneers will
broker that for a fee.

### 8. Ag lenders and bank trust departments
Farm Credit Services of America offices, community bank ag lenders, and bank
trust officers administer land in estates and know who's carrying
uncomfortable debt. They can't share client information, but they can pass
our one-pager to a client who'd benefit. Give them something professional to
hand over.

### 9. Estate and real estate attorneys in the county seat
Retain a local attorney early anyway (you'll need one for title and options).
Pick one who does estate work — they know every family situation in the
county and lend instant credibility: a letter on local letterhead
outperforms one from an out-of-state LLC.

### 10. Tenants as intermediaries
The operator farming a parcel usually isn't the owner (over half of Iowa
farmland is rented). Tenants know the owner's intentions and can broker an
introduction — especially if the structure lets them keep farming until
construction (see leaseback note in the assemblage doc). A tenant who sees
upside becomes an advocate instead of an obstacle.

## Tier 3 — Community and institutional channels

### 11. County economic development offices
Call the county EDC director before calling landowners. They want the tax
base, they know which owners are "sale-curious," they know the supervisors'
temperature on energy projects, and some counties maintain certified site
inventories that never hit any listing service. This also plants the seed
for the eventual permitting conversation.

### 12. Utilities and RECs
MidAmerican, Alliant, ITC Midwest, and the rural electric cooperatives have
siting and key-account teams. An energy campus is load/interconnection they
generally want. They sometimes know of parcels near their own substations
that owners have offered up, and early utility engagement is needed for the
interconnection story anyway.

### 13. Existing energy developers' castoffs
Wind and solar developers optioned enormous acreage across Iowa; plenty of
those options expired or projects died in the MISO queue. Landowners who
already signed one energy option are pre-educated and pre-disposed. County
recorder searches for recorded (and since-expired) wind/solar easements and
memoranda of option produce a literal list of energy-friendly owners.

### 14. Repurposing distressed industrial sites
Closed or struggling ethanol plants, shuttered grain facilities, former
power plant sites, and idle rail-served industrial parks come with acreage,
interconnection history, water, and a community that already accepted heavy
use. Check the Iowa Economic Development Authority's certified sites list
and just drive the transmission corridors.

## Tier 4 — Creative plays

### 15. The "section-line letter" campaign
Rather than blanketing a county, mail only owners inside the screened
clusters — 30 to 80 letters per cluster, personalized with their parcel's
legal description ("your farm in Section 14 of ___ Township"). Specificity
signals seriousness and gets 5–10x the response of generic "we buy land"
mail. Templates in [outreach-scripts.md](outreach-scripts.md).

### 16. Plat book + coffee shop circuit
Buy the county plat book (Farm & Home Publishers), then spend two days in
the county: co-op elevator, sale barn café, implement dealer. Introduce
yourself honestly as evaluating the area for a potential energy campus.
Rural Iowa's information network is extraordinary — within a week, people
who might sell will be calling *you*. This only works if the story you tell
is one you're happy to have repeated, which is another reason to keep it
truthful.

### 17. "Keep farming it" offers
Lead with the structure, not just price: an option payment now, a closing
years away, and the owner (or their tenant) farms it rent-free until
construction. For an owner on the fence, income-now-plus-farm-it-anyway
beats a plain purchase offer at a higher headline number.

### 18. Land swaps
Some owners won't sell the home place but would trade. Partnering with a
farm manager or Peoples Company-style brokerage that can source replacement
ground (including 1031 exchange structures) unlocks sellers a cash offer
never will. Mention 1031 capability in every conversation with an owner who
hesitates on taxes — capital gains fear kills more Iowa land deals than
price does.

### 19. Township trustees and Farm Bureau chapter officers
Elected township trustees and county Farm Bureau officers are the informal
mayors of rural townships. A respectful heads-up call ("we're looking at the
area, wanted you to hear it from us first") earns goodwill and almost always
ends with "you know, you ought to talk to ___."

---

## Sequencing

1. Weeks 1–2: build parcel universe, screen clusters, rank owners (Tier 1).
2. Week 2: retain local attorney + one broker/farm-manager channel (Tier 2).
3. Week 3: EDC + utility conversations (Tier 3), letters drop for cluster #1.
4. Week 4+: calls follow letters by 7–10 days; coffee-shop circuit during
   the same window; iterate cluster by cluster.
